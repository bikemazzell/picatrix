title: Waning Moon
source: Picatrix (adapted), Book I chapter 4 and Book II chapter 3

The waning Moon is the doctrine's difficult case. When she wanes, Picatrix says, she reveals damage, setback, delay, and obstruction upon all the affairs of this world, and the waning is counted three ways at once: diminished in light, diminished in reckoning, and slow in her motion, so that a Moon proceeding less than twelve degrees a day is assimilated to the slowness of Saturn. A waning Moon facing Saturn becomes a serious misfortune, where a waxing one facing Mars is merely counted a misfortune. Yet the state has its proper uses: she is apt and congruent for every task of diminution, and the waning half, from the opposition through the last square to conjunction, is the assigned ground for pursuing the debts owed to you, for those who borrow and return, and for philosophers and truth-seekers. The text even allows one dark exception: conditions that spoil ordinary work suit the works of escape and theft, the prisoner struggling free of his master and the man taking what is not his.
